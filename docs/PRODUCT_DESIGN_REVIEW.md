# Product Design Review: Post-Job Retirement Decision Engine

Generated by /office-hours on 2026-05-18  
Repo: drawdown-modeling  
Status: DRAFT  
Mode: Product / startup

## One-Line Verdict

Portfolio Success Lab should not position itself as a general drawdown planner first. The sharper wedge is:

> I recently left my job. Can my family stay retired, and what exact spending cut or income bridge keeps us safe if bad markets hit early?

The current app has unusually deep modeling for a free browser tool. The product gap is not more model surface. The gap is turning model output into a decision someone can act on with a spouse.

## Target User

Primary first user:

- 44 years old
- Massachusetts household
- Two young children
- One spouse already stay-at-home
- Recently left a job
- Enough assets to maybe retire
- Has hard spending requirements and flexible categories
- Needs to decide whether to stay loose with spending, find another job, consult part-time, or fully retire

This user is in a high-anxiety decision window. They do not primarily need a dashboard. They need an answer, a fallback plan, and reasons they can inspect.

## Product Thesis

The suite of fi-nance.com tools should eventually share household, asset, spending, tax, and account inputs. But the first tool needs to earn the right to become the shared-data foundation.

The drawdown app is the best first wedge because it can answer a scary, high-value question:

> If things go badly early, what do we change before it is too late?

CoastFI is a good second tool because it can reuse most of the same household, spending, assets, target-age, and income-bridge inputs. Credit-card transaction optimization is a later product because it requires bank/transaction permissions, merchant classification, card benefit data, and a different trust model.

## Current Product Review

### What Works

The core model is already far past a toy calculator.

- The README documents Monte Carlo, historical backtesting, tax-aware withdrawals, ACA, IRMAA, RMDs, setup import/export, and year-by-year cash-flow views in [README.md](../README.md).
- The app already has personas, outcomes, and a three-screen flow in [src/redesign.mjs](../src/redesign.mjs).
- The engine already has essential/discretionary guardrail inputs, sequence-risk reserve inputs, lifetime withdrawal optimization, ACA, and tax-efficiency settings in [src/core/simulation.mjs](../src/core/simulation.mjs).
- The results UI already surfaces Monte Carlo success, historical success, lifetime tax, healthcare, safe spend rate, and modeled years in [src/redesign.mjs](../src/redesign.mjs).
- The action plan already emits tax and cash-flow moves such as MAGI management, Roth conversions, RMDs, earned income, discretionary trims, harvesting, taxes, medical reserves, and unfunded gaps in [src/app.mjs](../src/app.mjs).

The model depth is a real asset. Most competitors either hide this in paid tiers or expose so much machinery that the user has to interpret it alone.

### What Is Not Working Yet

The current first screen asks broad planner questions:

- FIRE / early
- 5 years out
- Just retired
- Medicare-age
- Late stage
- Build my own

Those are reasonable presets, but they do not name the user's urgent moment. The first user we identified is not thinking, "I need a decumulation planner." They are thinking, "I left my job. Do I need to find another one?"

The current outcome choices are also broad:

- Will my money last?
- How much can I safely spend?
- Should I do Roth conversions?
- Which account should I draw from first?
- How do I keep ACA subsidies?

Those are understandable questions, but they do not change the user's plan by themselves. They split one emotional decision into five separate views. For the target user, these are sub-answers to one job-to-be-done:

> Tell me whether I can stay retired, and what I should do if the answer is fragile.

### Dogfood Observation

I ran the sample flow locally. A completed sample run showed:

- 54% Monte Carlo success
- 100% historical success
- $147k median ending value
- $0 worst 5% ending value
- $8.1M median historical ending value
- $842k worst historical path
- This-year action plan with taxable sales, ACA MAGI cap, gain harvesting, and loss harvesting

This is exactly the trust problem. A normal user sees "54%" and "100%" at the same time and needs interpretation:

- Is this plan safe or unsafe?
- Which number should I believe?
- What failed in the Monte Carlo paths?
- How much spending flexibility fixes the failures?
- Would a part-time job for 2 years fix it?
- Is the danger sequence risk, healthcare, taxes, or allocation?

The app has data. The product needs a decision layer.

## Landscape Findings

### ProjectionLab

ProjectionLab already offers a free Basic tier with ad hoc planning, cash-flow projections, Monte Carlo simulations, historical backtesting, and flexible modeling. Premium adds tax estimation, advanced visualizations, what-if scenarios, Roth conversions, tax analytics, compare mode, flex spending, and downloadable reports.

Implication: "ProjectionLab but free" is not a strong enough wedge. ProjectionLab already has a free tier, and users who value polish/trust may pay for Premium.

Source: https://projectionlab.com/pricing

### Boldin

Boldin's free tier offers basic planning. PlannerPlus adds state and federal tax projections, Roth conversion explorer, side-by-side scenarios, Monte Carlo, detailed budgeting, healthcare cost estimates, and many charts.

Implication: "complete planning suite" is a crowded product shape. The first version must win by being more specific, not broader.

Source: https://www.boldin.com/retirement/pricing/

### FI Calc / cFIREsim / Free FIRE Calculators

Free historical simulators are common. FI Calc focuses on historical simulation and withdrawal strategies. cFIREsim exposes many historical simulation controls. Newer free tools increasingly claim Monte Carlo, tax awareness, and no-account usage.

Implication: "free retirement calculator" is a noisy category. The trust edge comes from explaining failure modes and rescue actions better than general calculators.

Sources:

- https://guide.ficalc.app/
- https://cfiresim.com/

### Massachusetts ConnectorCare

Massachusetts is a real wedge, not a footnote. For 2026, ConnectorCare and Advance Premium Tax Credit eligibility have important income thresholds around 400% FPL, and families above the stated family-size limits can lose subsidies. This matters directly for the target user.

Implication: a Massachusetts post-job-retirement preset can feel much more useful than a generic ACA toggle.

Sources:

- https://betterhealthconnector.com/help-center/resource-download-center/2026-consumer-guide-to-subsidies
- https://www.mass.gov/technical-information-release/tir-26-1-individual-mandate-penalties-for-tax-year-2026

### Guardrails

Dynamic spending guardrails are a known retirement planning idea: pre-decide when spending should be cut or raised based on portfolio stress or success probability. The key user value is deciding the rule before emotion takes over.

Implication: this app should not merely support guardrails as an advanced control. Guardrails should be the core language of the first wedge.

Source: https://www.kitces.com/blog/probability-of-success-driven-guardrails-advantages-monte-carlo-simulations-analysis-communication/

## Premises

1. The first wedge is the post-job household decision, not a broad financial suite.
2. The first answer should be the bad-market contingency answer, not a raw success rate.
3. Spending flexibility is the main product mechanic for the target user.
4. Massachusetts healthcare and tax specificity can be a trust advantage.
5. More tax-modeling depth only helps if the app makes assumptions visible and explains what changed the answer.
6. The suite should grow from shared inputs only after this first tool earns repeated use.

## Product Positioning

### Current Positioning

Tax-aware retirement decumulation forecasting with Monte Carlo simulation, historical backtesting, and Sankey cash-flow visualization.

This describes the machinery.

### Recommended Positioning

Free retirement decision tools for households making real money decisions.

First tool:

> Recently left work? See whether you can stay retired, what breaks first, and the exact spending cut or income bridge that keeps the plan alive.

This describes the user's moment.

## Recommended Product Direction

Build a decision layer on top of the current drawdown model.

Do not start by rebuilding the model. Do not start by adding the CoastFI calculator. Do not start by adding transaction imports. The next product work should make the current app answer the user's real question.

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Minimal Viable Repositioning

Summary: Rename first-screen personas and outcome choices around the post-job decision. Add a "Recently left work" persona and a "Bad market fallback" outcome that pre-enables healthcare, strategy, what-ifs, and guardrails.

Effort: Small  
Risk: Low

Pros:

- Fastest path to a sharper first impression.
- Reuses the existing three-screen flow.
- Lets you test the wedge before building solvers.

Cons:

- Does not yet calculate the rescue answer.
- Still risks dumping users into the existing dashboard.
- May feel like copy polish if the result screen does not change.

Reuses:

- Persona presets in `src/redesign.mjs`
- Outcome focus routing in `src/redesign.mjs`
- Existing spending guardrail controls

### Approach B: Decision Engine Over Current App

Summary: Add a result layer that translates model outputs into "base plan," "first thing that breaks," "spending cut needed," "income bridge needed," and "tax/ACA threshold to avoid."

Effort: Medium  
Risk: Medium

Pros:

- Directly answers the target user's actual question.
- Turns failed paths into useful alternatives instead of fear.
- Creates a differentiated product experience competitors do not lead with.

Cons:

- Requires solver logic and careful language.
- Needs clear confidence labels so users do not mistake it for advice.
- Requires more QA because recommendations can change behavior.

Reuses:

- Current Monte Carlo scenarios
- Historical backtests
- Action plan rows
- Spending guardrail fields
- One-off income fields
- ACA MAGI ceiling fields
- Current-year and all-year ledgers

### Approach C: Suite Platform First

Summary: Build fi-nance.com as a shared-input platform where drawdown, CoastFI, and credit-card optimization live side-by-side.

Effort: Large  
Risk: High

Pros:

- Matches the long-term vision.
- Shared household/account inputs can compound across tools.
- Creates a broader brand surface.

Cons:

- Too wide before one tool has earned trust.
- Credit-card transactions introduce a different privacy and data model.
- Platform navigation can hide the one sharp answer the first user needs.

Reuses:

- Setup backup/restore format
- Portfolio importers
- Household and spending inputs

### Selected Direction

Approach B is the plan. Approach A can be folded into it as the first implementation step. Approach C should wait until the drawdown decision engine has been tested with real users.

## Design: Post-Job Retirement Decision Engine

### Problem Statement

A household has recently lost or left employment and may have enough assets to retire early. They need to decide whether they can remain retired, how loose they can keep spending, and whether they need to find another job or part-time income.

Current retirement tools make this hard because they either:

- ask for a full planning setup before giving an answer,
- show a probability without an action,
- hide tax and healthcare mechanics,
- or expose so many controls that the user has to become the planner.

### Core User Question

> Can we stay retired, and what do we change if the first few years go badly?

### Front-Door Result

The first result should not be only "54% success."

It should be a math-backed sentence like:

> Base plan: 54% success at $90k/year. Cutting discretionary spending by $18k during early market stress raises this to 78%. Earning $42k/year for 3 years raises it to 86%. Doing both raises it to 93%.

The exact numbers should come from rerunning comparable scenarios with the same seeds and assumptions. The sentence is the headline, but the product must show the math directly under it:

- Base plan success rate
- Each recommended change
- Success-rate delta for each change
- Combined success rate when changes are stacked
- Historical worst path
- First failure year cluster
- ACA/ConnectorCare MAGI ceiling
- This year's recommended moves

This keeps the experience decisive without hiding the math. The user sees both the plain-English action and the numerical reason it matters.

### Core Concepts

#### Required Spending

Spending that cannot be cut without breaking the household plan:

- housing
- groceries
- insurance
- healthcare premiums and expected out-of-pocket costs
- child expenses
- taxes
- debt payments

#### Flexible Spending

Spending that can be trimmed under stress:

- travel
- restaurants
- entertainment
- upgrades
- optional shopping
- gifts
- hobby spend

#### Income Bridge

Optional earned income that can rescue a fragile plan:

- consulting
- part-time work
- returning to a lower-paying job
- one spouse returning temporarily
- short fixed contract

#### Failure Mode

The first major reason the plan becomes unsafe:

- bad early returns
- spending too high
- ACA/ConnectorCare subsidy loss
- taxes from poor withdrawal order
- too much portfolio risk
- too little portfolio risk
- cash reserve exhausted too soon

### New First-Screen Flow

Replace broad persona language with situation language.

Recommended choices:

1. Recently left work
   - "Can we stay retired, or do I need income?"
   - Defaults: age 44, spouse 44, 50-year plan, Massachusetts visible in state suggestions, healthcare enabled, guardrails enabled.

2. One more year syndrome
   - "Would one more year materially change the answer?"
   - Defaults: pre-retirement income bridge controls enabled.

3. Coast with part-time income
   - "How much income buys enough safety?"
   - Defaults: earned-income bridge controls enabled.

4. Healthcare bridge
   - "Can we manage ACA or ConnectorCare until Medicare?"
   - Defaults: healthcare, ACA plan helper, MAGI thresholds enabled.

5. Build my own
   - Existing custom path.

### Outcome Choices

Do not make outcome choices the main front door for the post-job flow. They are useful as secondary lenses after the main decision answer exists, but they should not block the user before the tool has answered the core question.

The primary post-job flow should have one default objective:

> Can we stay retired, and what change makes the plan safe enough if early markets are bad?

Then expose the old outcome choices as tabs, filters, or follow-up cards:

1. Bad-market fallback
2. Safe spending boundary
3. Income bridge
4. Tax and healthcare actions
5. Assumptions that move the answer

For other personas, outcome choices can remain useful. For the "Recently left work" path, preselect the bad-market fallback and let the user switch lenses after they see the verdict.

### New Results Hierarchy

#### Panel 1: Decision

One top card:

- Verdict: safe, fragile, or unsafe
- Base success rate
- Main condition: "success rises from X% to Y% if flexible spending can fall to $Z"
- Confidence: Monte Carlo and historical evidence summarized with any disagreement explained
- Next action: "Set your bad-market rule now"

Default objective:

> Do not run out of money.

The verdict should optimize for avoiding portfolio depletion first. Once that floor is met, the app should prefer the lowest-disruption rescue option: smaller spending cut, shorter income bridge, lower MAGI disruption, or less allocation churn. "No need to return to full-time work" is an output, not the primary objective.

#### Panel 2: Rescue Options

Show ranked options:

1. Cut discretionary spending by `$A` during early market stress.
2. Earn `$B/year` for `Z` years.
3. Keep MAGI below `$C` to preserve healthcare subsidy.
4. Shift allocation to target `D/E` only if it improves failure paths without hurting worst historical paths.
5. Hold `N` years of required spending in reserve.

Each option should show:

- success rate before
- success rate after
- percentage-point improvement
- historical worst path before
- historical worst path after
- annual lifestyle cost
- tax/healthcare side effects

#### Panel 3: Failure Anatomy

Turn failed simulations into clusters:

- earliest failure year
- median failure year
- most common failure trigger
- market drawdown at failure
- spending level at failure
- MAGI/healthcare status at failure

Do not make users read 250 scenario rows to understand failure.

#### Panel 4: This Year's Plan

Keep the current action plan, but put it below the decision cards.

Add plain-language grouping:

- Cash to raise
- Taxes to reserve
- MAGI guardrail
- Healthcare plan
- Conversions or harvesting
- Spending rule

#### Panel 5: Audit Trail

Keep the model audit. This is a trust feature. But position it as "Why this answer changed" and "What assumptions matter."

### Solver Requirements

The decision engine needs five solver families.

#### Solver 1: Safe Spending Boundary

Input:

- current assets
- household setup
- required spending
- flexible spending
- tax/healthcare settings
- target success threshold, defaulting to 90% and user-overridable

Output:

- max annual spending at target threshold
- required/flexible split
- historical worst-path floor
- first-year action plan at that spending level

Implementation sketch:

- binary search total spending
- preserve required spending floor
- cut flexible spending first
- re-run model with fixed seeds for comparable before/after

#### Solver 2: Bad-Market Discretionary Cut

Input:

- current spending split
- drawdown thresholds
- target success threshold

Output:

- minimum flexible spending cut in bad early-market paths
- years when cut is likely needed
- success-rate delta
- worst-path delta

Implementation sketch:

- use existing essential/discretionary guardrail fields
- sweep correction and bear discretionary percentages
- compare against base plan
- surface smallest cut that reaches threshold

#### Solver 3: Income Bridge

Input:

- income start year
- duration range
- annual income range
- tax treatment
- target retirement age or target success threshold

Output:

- minimum annual income and duration
- best timing
- tax and ACA impact
- "job urgency" interpretation

Implementation sketch:

- reuse one-off income / earned income mechanics
- sweep income values and durations
- prefer shorter duration only if annual income is plausible
- penalize income that destroys ACA/ConnectorCare subsidies without improving plan enough

#### Solver 4: MAGI / Healthcare Threshold

Input:

- state
- household size
- marketplace members
- current MAGI
- planned gains/conversions/withdrawals

Output:

- MAGI ceiling
- current buffer
- subsidy at risk
- moves that consume MAGI room
- moves to defer

Implementation sketch:

- reuse ACA fields and MA ConnectorCare helper
- auto-populate available plan choices where supported, then let the user choose the plan
- allow exact manual overrides for premiums, SLCSP, out-of-pocket maximum, and plan metadata
- explicitly label 2026 threshold source
- show "do not cross" amounts in this-year plan

The ideal Massachusetts flow should not require users to manually enter exact plan quotes. The app should fill reasonable plan choices where data access allows it, ask the user to choose, and keep manual override controls for edge cases or stale data.

#### Solver 5: Evidence Weighting

Input:

- Monte Carlo summary
- historical backtest summary
- historical worst path
- selected rescue option deltas

Output:

- combined verdict
- Monte Carlo interpretation
- historical interpretation
- disagreement warning when one passes and the other fails

Implementation sketch:

- weight Monte Carlo and historical evidence equally by default
- treat "Monte Carlo fragile, historical passes" as fragile, not safe
- treat "Monte Carlo passes, historical fragile" as fragile, not safe
- show both scores side by side so the user can decide which evidence they trust more

Monte Carlo and historical backtests both have merit. Monte Carlo explores modeled uncertainty. Historical backtests ground the plan in real sequence paths. The product should avoid collapsing them into one opaque score.

### Data Model Additions

Add a decision profile to saved setup:

```json
{
  "decisionProfile": {
    "mode": "recentlyLeftWork",
    "requiredSpend": 72000,
    "flexibleSpend": 28000,
    "targetSuccessRate": 0.9,
    "targetSuccessRateUserOverridden": false,
    "verdictObjective": "avoidDepletion",
    "minimumAcceptableHistoricalWorstEnding": 0,
    "evidenceWeights": {
      "monteCarlo": 0.5,
      "historical": 0.5
    },
    "incomeBridge": {
      "enabled": true,
      "startYear": 2026,
      "maxYears": 5,
      "incomeType": "medicareWages"
    },
    "healthcarePriority": "preserveConnectorCare",
    "healthcarePlanSelection": {
      "mode": "autoPopulateWithManualOverride",
      "selectedPlanId": null
    },
    "share": {
      "mode": "editableResultLink",
      "allowInputTweaks": true
    }
  }
}
```

Keep this separate from low-level scenario fields so future fi-nance.com tools can reuse the household decision intent without inheriting every drawdown knob.

### Shared fi-nance.com Platform Direction

The long-term shared-input model should have these layers:

1. Household profile
   - ages
   - filing status
   - dependents
   - state
   - healthcare status

2. Money profile
   - assets
   - account types
   - cost basis
   - income sources
   - recurring spending

3. Decision profile
   - retirement target
   - risk tolerance
   - required/flexible spending split
   - job/income flexibility

4. Tool-specific scenarios
   - drawdown
   - CoastFI
   - Roth conversion
   - ACA/ConnectorCare
   - credit-card optimization later

Do not make transaction import part of the first shared-data foundation. Transaction data belongs to a later "spending intelligence" layer after the retirement decision tool earns trust.

## Action Plan

### Phase 1: Reposition And User Test

Goal: validate the wedge before adding solver complexity.

Tasks:

1. Add "Recently left work" as the primary first-screen path.
2. Add "What happens if markets drop early?" as the primary outcome.
3. Make Massachusetts visible in the first-run experience for this path.
4. Add required/flexible spending fields to the basics or strategy module.
5. Create a sample scenario matching the 44-year-old Massachusetts household.
6. Rewrite top result copy from "Money lasts in X%" to a decision sentence.
7. Run one user test with the target archetype.

Acceptance criteria:

- A first-time user can state the app's answer in one sentence.
- A first-time user can identify the fallback action.
- A first-time user can find the assumptions that changed the answer.

### Phase 2: Bad-Market Guardrail Solver

Goal: make answer #3 the front door.

Tasks:

1. Build a solver that sweeps discretionary spending cuts.
2. Compare base plan vs guardrail plan using the same seed set.
3. Add a "bad early markets" result card.
4. Cluster failed scenarios by first failure year and market drawdown.
5. Add action rows for required discretionary cuts.

Acceptance criteria:

- Results show the minimum discretionary cut needed to reach the target threshold.
- Results explain how often the cut is triggered.
- Results show whether historical worst paths survive.

### Phase 3: Income Bridge Solver

Goal: answer "how important is it to find another job?"

Tasks:

1. Sweep income amount and duration.
2. Support full-time, part-time, and consulting labels.
3. Account for tax and ACA/ConnectorCare side effects.
4. Rank bridge options by lowest life disruption.
5. Add "job urgency" verdict.

Acceptance criteria:

- User sees the minimum income bridge that changes the decision.
- User can compare "cut spending" vs "earn income" vs "do both."
- User sees if income breaks healthcare subsidies.

### Phase 4: Massachusetts Healthcare Trust Pass

Goal: make the first wedge feel local and trustworthy.

Tasks:

1. Add a Massachusetts post-job setup preset.
2. Show ConnectorCare / APTC threshold summary in plain English.
3. Link the modeled threshold to official source notes.
4. Warn when subsidy assumptions are missing exact plan inputs.
5. Add a "healthcare cliff" sensitivity card.

Acceptance criteria:

- Massachusetts users can see whether MAGI is above or below the relevant threshold.
- The app distinguishes estimated premiums from exact plan inputs.
- The user sees what move would accidentally cross the threshold.

### Phase 5: Shareable Results Links

Goal: make the spouse review real.

Tasks:

1. Add a share link that opens the final results with the full scenario state.
2. Let the recipient inspect and tweak inputs in the app.
3. Preserve the original result as "shared baseline" so changes can be compared.
4. Add a compact spouse-readable summary above the editable controls.
5. Make privacy explicit: local-only encoded state first, account-backed saved scenarios later if needed.

Acceptance criteria:

- A spouse can open the link and understand the verdict before touching inputs.
- A spouse can change spending, income bridge, or plan assumptions and see the math update.
- The app shows what changed from the shared baseline.

### Phase 6: Suite Foundation

Goal: prepare for CoastFI without turning the app into a platform too early.

Tasks:

1. Define shared household and money profile schemas.
2. Keep drawdown scenario settings separate from reusable household inputs.
3. Add a reusable setup export format for future tools.
4. Prototype CoastFI using the same household/assets/spending inputs.
5. Only then revisit site-level navigation for multiple tools.

Acceptance criteria:

- CoastFI can consume the same household and asset profile.
- A user does not need to re-enter basics across tools.
- Tool-specific assumptions remain visible and isolated.

## User Test Script

Use one person who resembles the target user. Do not demo. Let them use it.

Setup:

- Give them a sample or their own anonymized numbers.
- Ask them to choose the "recently left work" path.
- Ask them to read the result out loud.

Questions:

1. What do you think the app is telling you to do?
2. Would you feel safer, less safe, or confused after seeing this?
3. What number would you show your spouse?
4. What assumption do you most want to challenge?
5. If markets dropped 25% next year, what would you do based on this result?
6. Would you trust this enough to delay looking for work by one month?

Pass condition:

The user can explain the fallback plan without reading the ledger.

Fail condition:

The user says "I need to understand the model better" before they can name an action.

## Success Metrics

### Product Metrics

- Time to first verdict: under 5 minutes with sample data
- Time to first personal verdict: under 20 minutes with user data
- Percent of users who can name their fallback action after one run
- Percent of users who export or share a spouse-readable summary
- Percent of users who run a rescue option after seeing a fragile result

### Modeling Metrics

- Solver result reproducibility with fixed seed
- Difference between base and rescue plans displayed consistently
- Failure clusters explain at least 80% of failed paths by primary driver
- Healthcare threshold warnings appear whenever exact plan data is missing

### Trust Metrics

- User can identify the top three assumptions
- User can see data sources for tax and healthcare facts
- User understands the difference between Monte Carlo and historical results
- User knows this is a planning model, not tax advice

## Risks

### Risk 1: "Complete Tax Modeling" Overpromise

The user asked for complete tax modeling. That phrase is dangerous. The product should say:

> tax-aware planning model with visible assumptions

Trust comes from being explicit about what is modeled, what is estimated, and what requires external verification.

### Risk 2: Probability Confusion

Monte Carlo and historical backtests can disagree. The UI needs an interpretation layer:

- Monte Carlo asks "what could happen under this return model?"
- Historical asks "what would have happened in past sequences?"
- The decision engine asks "what action keeps both acceptable?"

### Risk 3: Feature Sprawl

CoastFI and credit-card optimization are attractive, but they can distract from proving the first wedge.

Rule:

> Add the next tool only when the first one produces a shareable decision users understand.

### Risk 4: Healthcare Accuracy

For the target user, healthcare can dominate the decision. Missing exact plan inputs or using stale thresholds can create false confidence.

Mitigation:

- source-linked threshold notes
- exact-plan input prompts
- visible "estimate" labels
- annual data refresh checklist

## Resolved Product Defaults

1. Default success threshold: 90%, with a visible user override.
2. Verdict objective: optimize first for not running out of money. After that, prefer the lowest-disruption rescue option.
3. Massachusetts healthcare: auto-populate plan choices where possible, let the user choose, and keep exact manual overrides.
4. Evidence weighting: Monte Carlo and historical paths are equally weighted by default. If one passes and the other is fragile, the verdict should stay fragile and explain the disagreement.
5. Spouse sharing: use a shareable result link that opens the final results and allows the recipient to inspect and tweak inputs in the app.

## The Assignment

Put this in front of one target user and ask them to explain the answer back to you.

Do not ask whether they like it. Ask what they would do next.

If they cannot name the fallback action after seeing the result, the next build step is not more tax depth. It is the decision layer.
